## Abstract

AI agents increasingly begin work in one session and leave it to be continued in another session, by another agent, another model, or a human. What crosses that boundary today is usually a natural language summary, a raw transcript or journal, or nothing at all. Summaries compress away exactly the governance content a successor needs most: which decisions were made and why, which statements are assumptions rather than facts and with what confidence, which decisions have been superseded, and which constraints still bind. Transcripts preserve more, but index none of it. Neither format can be checked mechanically for constraint violations or for later editing.

This paper presents WORLD, a research prototype of a persistent work-state primitive intended to test whether an explicit, structured, and verifiable record does better. In WORLD, one named world is one SQLite file. Writers propose whole-document JSON updates. Each proposal is judged against declared invariants. The verdict, COMMITTED or REJECTED, is appended either way to a SHA-256 hash-chained log. Optimistic concurrency, idempotency keys, checkpoints, signed tips, per-field encryption, Merkle inclusion proofs, and a typed handoff layer are built on that log. The core uses only the Python standard library.

The paper makes three contributions, each stated at the strength the evidence supports. First, it describes the architecture and its security boundary, including the limits that testing pins down rather than hides. Second, it reports a mechanized verification of the commit protocol: an explicit-state model checker explored 1,220,255 states over three bounded configurations and found properties P1 to P6 holding on every reachable state, with a mutation test producing the expected counterexample when a core safeguard is removed (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md; spec/model_check.py). Model checking of this kind is not testing, and Section 5 states the distinction precisely. Third, it reports a line of handoff experiments. The strongest result is narrow and PRELIMINARY. In preregistered Experiment #003 (8 tasks, 3 conditions, 72 continuations, blind grading, information parity gate), a WORLD state bundle preserved assumption metadata better than a summary or a journal: metric 3 means were A 0.721, B 0.500, and C 0.488 (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md). Baselines usually recovered the substance of an assumption but lost its confidence level or its status as an assumption rather than a fact. The same experiment found no WORLD advantage on decisions, constraints, provenance, revision history, contradictions, or judged quality, found WORLD to be the least context-efficient condition on all 8 tasks, and found unverifiable additions only under the WORLD condition. A critical scope limit applies to all of Experiment #003: condition A artifacts were hand-authored Markdown following docs/state-model.md, so Experiment #003 never exercised the World class or any code path in src/world. A smaller Prompt 3 revalidation study is the first experiment line that did exercise the shipped code path, and its results are also PRELIMINARY.

The paper does not claim that WORLD prevents hallucinations, guarantees reliable AI, or improves agent reliability in general. That general claim is classified throughout as NEEDS VALIDATION.

## 1 Introduction

Long-running knowledge work does not fit inside a single context window or a single session. A research task, a software change, a financial analysis, or a planning exercise is started, paused, and resumed. The participant who resumes it is often not the participant who started it. It may be a different agent session, a different model family, a different tool, or a person. The handoff is therefore not an edge case. For any system that delegates work across time, the handoff is the normal case, and the quality of what crosses the boundary determines whether the successor continues the work or quietly restarts it under new labels, with lost constraints and reinvented decisions.

The conventional boundary objects are weak in complementary ways. A summary is readable and compact, but it is written once, by one author, with no schema and no check. Whether a decision survives as a decision, whether an assumption survives with its confidence, and whether a superseded decision is marked as superseded all depend on whether the summarizer happened to preserve those distinctions in prose. A transcript or journal preserves the raw sequence, but it buries governance content in chronological narrative. A successor must re-derive the current decisions, the open questions, and the binding constraints from text that was written to record activity, not to state current state. Neither object records rejected work as evidence. Neither object can answer, mechanically, whether a declared constraint still holds, or whether the record itself was edited after the fact.

WORLD starts from a different premise: the boundary object should be the shared state itself, maintained as data while the work proceeds, not reconstructed from prose after the work pauses. The state names decisions, assumptions, evidence, constraints, open questions, and next actions as labeled entities. Every proposed change to that state is judged against declared invariants before it enters the committed record, and the judgment itself, including rejections, becomes part of a tamper-evident log that a later participant can verify. The successor loads the same named document the predecessor wrote.

That premise is a hypothesis, not a result. The research statement in research/HYPOTHESIS.md puts it this way: for work that moves between participants across sessions, maintaining an explicit, persistent, structured work-state, with stable labels for decisions, assumptions, evidence, constraints, and revision history, and with every proposed change judged against declared invariants and recorded in a tamper-evident log, preserves more decision-relevant information across handoffs, and makes that information more auditable, than relying on conversation summaries or raw transcripts. This paper reports what happened when that hypothesis was built as a system and then tested, including the parts that failed.

Three cautions frame everything that follows. First, the handoff evidence is small. Experiments #001 and #002 used one continuation per condition per run (docs/experiment-001-results.md; docs/experiment-002/results.md). Experiment #003 improved the design to 3 trials per cell over 8 tasks, but remained single model family, agent graded, and small (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md). Second, the most cited experiment in this line, Experiment #003, tested a document schema expressed as hand-authored Markdown. It did not test the shipped persistence code. Conflating those two objects would overstate what was measured, and this paper keeps them separate in every section. Third, verification results about the commit protocol and experimental results about handoff quality are different kinds of evidence about different claims. A model-checked commit protocol does not imply that structured state helps a successor agent, and a promising handoff result does not imply that the underlying log is correct. The classification labels exist to keep those lines distinct.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formulates the cross-session handoff problem and states the research questions, hypotheses, and null hypotheses exactly as preregistered for Experiment #003. Section 3 positions WORLD against agent memory systems, summarization, event sourcing, durable execution, checkpointing, and provenance standards. Section 4 describes the architecture. Section 5 states the formal properties and what the model checker did and did not establish. Section 6 describes the experimental methodology for Experiments #002 and #003 and for the Prompt 3 revalidation study. Section 7 reports results from all experiment lines and from the systems evidence, with negative findings at equal prominence. Section 8 gives the security and threat model. Section 9 reports dated performance records. Section 10 collects limitations. Section 11 lists future work. Section 12 states precisely what the evidence establishes and what it does not.

## 2 Problem Formulation

### 2.1 The cross-session handoff problem

Define a work episode as a sequence of actions by one or more participants that produces decisions, relies on assumptions, cites evidence, operates under constraints, revises earlier positions, and leaves open questions and next actions. Define a handoff as the transfer of responsibility for that episode from a predecessor participant to a successor participant who did not witness the original episode, where the only information available to the successor is a boundary object prepared at or before the transfer. Define the three boundary objects compared in this research program as follows. A structured state bundle is a document that states the current work-state under explicit labels for each entity type. A summary is a natural language condensation of the episode, written without a prescribed schema. A journal or transcript is the chronological record of the episode, unedited or lightly edited.

The handoff problem is then a preservation problem under a format choice. For each decision-relevant item in the original episode, does the successor, working only from the boundary object, recover the item with the attributes that make it usable: for a decision, its statement, its rationale, and whether it is current or superseded; for an assumption, its statement, the fact that it is an assumption rather than an established fact, and its confidence; for evidence, its source; for a revision, the original position, the revised position, what changed, why it changed, and which version is now authoritative; for a constraint, its content and its binding force on next actions. A second part of the problem is behavioral: does the successor, working from the boundary object, avoid contradicting the record, avoid presenting invented material as inherited material, and produce a continuation judged to be good work. A third part is economic: how much context, measured in this program in words because no tokenizer was available in the experiment environment, does the boundary object cost the successor per unit of preservation.

A fourth part of the problem is not about the successor at all. It is about the record. Can a later participant, possibly much later and possibly adversarially motivated to doubt the record, check that the record was not surgically edited, that rejected proposals were preserved rather than erased, that concurrent writers did not silently overwrite each other, and that a named checkpoint still points at the state it was created to pin. WORLD treats this audit problem as part of the handoff problem, because a boundary object that cannot be checked asks the successor to trust the predecessor and every intermediary without evidence.

### 2.2 Research questions

The program addresses four research questions. RQ1, retention: does a successor given structured work-state preserve more decisions, assumptions, provenance, and revision history than a successor given a summary or a journal of the same work. RQ2, safety of continuation: does structured work-state reduce contradictions and unsupported inferences in the continuation, relative to the same baselines. RQ3, cost: what context cost does structured work-state impose per unit of preservation, relative to the same baselines. RQ4, record integrity: can the underlying record be built so that its stated integrity, concurrency, and judgment properties hold under mechanized checking, adversarial testing, fuzzing, and fault injection, and where exactly do those properties stop.

RQ1 to RQ3 are empirical questions about agent behavior and can only be answered by handoff experiments. RQ4 is a systems question and is answered by model checking, property testing, fuzzing, and fault injection. No answer to RQ4 substitutes for an answer to RQ1, and no answer to RQ1 substitutes for an answer to RQ4.

### 2.3 Hypotheses and null hypotheses

The hypotheses below are reproduced from the preregistration in experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, preregistered 2026-11-03T03:33Z before any Experiment #003 runs. They are stated here in the form in which they were registered, so that the verdicts in Section 7 can be read against the registered text rather than against a retrospective paraphrase.

Primary hypothesis. H1: Agent B continuations working from a WORLD state bundle (condition A) will score higher than continuations working from a natural language summary (condition B) or a raw journal (condition C) on retention of decisions, assumptions, provenance, and revision history, measured per metric, with no composite score.

Secondary hypotheses. H2: Condition A will show the largest advantage on revision history reconstruction (metric 5) and provenance (metric 4), the two areas where Experiment #002 found both the clearest WORLD advantage (provenance) and the clearest WORLD weakness (revision history, 2 of 5 in both Experiment #002 runs). H2 was registered as deliberately two-sided in interest, because metric 5 was identified in advance as the metric most likely to refute the WORLD thesis. H3: Condition A will produce fewer unsupported inferences (metric 7) and fewer contradictions (metric 6) than conditions B and C. H4: Constraint retention (metric 2) will be at or near ceiling in all conditions, as in Experiment #002, so condition A will show no meaningful advantage there. H5: On the low-governance task (C2), condition A will show no advantage over conditions B or C, and condition B will be the most context-efficient condition.

Null hypotheses and conditions under which WORLD may lose. N1, summary parity: a competent natural language summary preserves the graded content as well as the state bundle. N1 is falsifiability condition 1 from research/HYPOTHESIS.md, still open after Experiment #002, in which the summary finished within 0.36 of WORLD on the reference mean in the Disney replication (docs/experiment-002/results.md). N2, transcript advantage on revisions: a raw journal preserves revision narrative, meaning what happened and in what order, better than a state projection that emphasizes current state. N3, structure overhead without benefit: on tasks with few decisions and no revisions, the state bundle costs more words for the same preservation. N4, writing bottleneck: the predecessor may fail to record revision chains in the state bundle even when the model can express them, in which case a metric 5 failure is a state-writing failure rather than a representation failure. The grading design for Experiment #003 included failure-cause coding to distinguish N4 from a data model failure, although in the event no revision chain failures occurred and no failure-cause codes arose (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md).

The broader falsifiability conditions from research/HYPOTHESIS.md remain part of the problem formulation. Summary parity is N1 above. Tool sufficiency asks whether Git plus a documents folder plus a tool protocol achieves the same handoff fidelity with less complexity, and is untested. Ontology collapse asks whether the work-state schema can be defined generically enough to transfer across projects, and is untested beyond the task genres used so far. Verification irrelevance asks whether anyone audits provenance in practice, and is untested, because no human audit exercise has been run.


## 3 Related Work

This section distinguishes two kinds of relationship. Some systems are comparable to WORLD in purpose, meaning they also persist information for agents across time, and a reader could reasonably ask why WORLD should exist instead of them. Other work supplies mechanisms or vocabularies that WORLD borrows, and for those the honest claim is application and combination, not novelty. In particular, this paper claims no novelty over event sourcing. WORLD's log is an application of event sourcing ideas to agent work-state, with judging and verdict recording as the differentiators, stated carefully below. The positioning in this section follows research/RELATED_WORK.md and research/comparisons.md.

### 3.1 Agent memory systems

A large product and research category persists context across sessions for an agent. The reference academic design is MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023), which treats the context window as one tier in a memory hierarchy that the model itself pages in and out of, drawing on the operating system analogy of virtual memory [1]. Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) is the adjacent landmark for memory streams plus reflection: agents store a complete record of experiences in natural language, synthesize higher-level reflections over time, and retrieve memories dynamically to plan behavior, in service of believable simulated behavior [2]. The broader category described in research/RELATED_WORK.md includes systems that organize memory as a temporal knowledge graph, systems that extract and consolidate facts from conversations into a retrievable store, systems that build knowledge graphs from ingested documents, and frameworks that persist agent state and memory blocks as server-side objects. Those product descriptions are given in research/RELATED_WORK.md as descriptions of publicly documented approaches, without formal citations, and this paper does not elevate them to cited claims.

What memory systems of this kind optimize for is recall by an agent: getting the right fragments back in front of a model at the right time. What WORLD targets instead is different in four respects. First, a canonical, model-agnostic record of a project's work-state that is the same object for every participant, rather than a per-agent or per-framework store. Second, work semantics as schema: decisions with rationale, assumptions with confidence, supersession, constraints, and open questions as first-class labeled entities rather than retrieved text fragments. Third, write-time judgment: proposed changes validated against declared invariants before they enter the record, with rejections preserved. Fourth, verifiable history: a hash-chained log that a third party can check without trusting the writer. The boundary is equally important in the other direction. WORLD has no retrieval layer, no embeddings, and no automatic fact extraction. A memory system answers the question of what is relevant now. WORLD answers the question of what the agreed state of the work is and how it got that way. The two are composable, not substitutes.

Two neighboring agent frameworks clarify the boundary further. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) interleaves reasoning traces and task-specific actions so that reasoning helps a model induce, track, and update action plans while actions let it gather information from external sources [6]. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) reinforces language agents through linguistic feedback: an agent reflects verbally on task feedback and maintains reflective text in an episodic memory buffer to improve later trials, without updating model weights [5]. Both are comparable to WORLD only at the level of agent architecture. Neither is a shared, verifiable work-state record. ReAct is a pattern for acting within a task. Reflexion is a pattern for learning across trials of a task. WORLD is a record that outlives any single acting or learning loop and can be handed to a participant who ran neither.

### 3.2 Conversation summarization

Summarization is the conventional alternative and the control condition in WORLD's own experiments: condense the prior conversation or work log into prose and hand the successor the summary. Framework support is widespread, including rolling summary buffers and hierarchical schemes that summarize older material while keeping recent material verbatim. Summarization is cheap, readable, and model-portable, and its strength must be stated plainly because the experiments in this paper depend on it: in Experiment #002, a strong independently written summary came within 0.36 of the WORLD condition on the reference mean in the Disney replication (docs/experiment-002/results.md). Any account that treats the summary baseline as weak misrepresents the record.

The structural weaknesses that WORLD is built against are properties of the format, not of any particular summarizer. A summary is written once, by one author, with no schema, so decision status, confidence, and supersession survive only if the author happens to preserve them. It cannot be mechanically verified against its source. It carries no write-time constraint checking. Each new handoff re-summarizes, compounding loss. WORLD's claim is not that summaries are poor. It is that summaries are unverifiable and unjudged, and that whether their informality costs anything measurable is an empirical question. The falsifiability condition of summary parity, N1 in Section 2, exists precisely because the summary baseline is strong.

### 3.3 Event sourcing

Event sourcing is a mechanism WORLD borrows, not a competitor WORLD displaces. In the formulation by Fowler (2005), event sourcing captures all changes to an application state as a sequence of events, so that past states can be reconstructed and the event log can serve as a foundation for adjusting state to cope with retroactive changes [3]. WORLD deliberately adopts the core mechanics: an append-only log, current state understood in relation to history, and reconstruction of earlier states.

The differences are in vocabulary, in what is logged, and in the boundary the log is designed to cross. Domain events in event-sourced systems are meaningful inside one application's bounded context. WORLD's log records proposals and verdicts about a whole work document, COMMITTED or REJECTED, with the judgment itself, including which invariant failed and why in the recorded note and status, as part of the record. One further difference matters for implementation honesty: the current WORLD primitive stores the complete document in every committed row, so retrieving an earlier state is snapshot retrieval, not event replay. The legacy engine in src/world_engine does provide deterministic replay for its own entity tables, but the primitive in src/world has no replay function and does not need one for state reconstruction (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section B5). WORLD therefore adds no new storage theory and claims none. The delta, if any, is the verdict semantics and the handoff-oriented state model, not the log.

### 3.4 Persistent and durable execution, and checkpointing

Durable execution systems persist the execution state of a program or workflow so that a crashed or long-running process resumes where it stopped. Agent graph frameworks provide checkpointers that snapshot thread state at step boundaries so a run can resume, branch, or be inspected. These systems persist execution state: where the program is. WORLD persists work state: what has been decided, assumed, and left open, and why. An orchestrator resumes a crashed run of the same program. WORLD briefs a new participant, possibly a different model in a different tool, who was never part of the original run. The overlap is real, since both families are append-friendly and history-aware, and a serious deployment would plausibly use both layers. WORLD does not compete on scheduling, retries, timers, or exactly-once activity execution, and claims no advantage there. This comparison is drawn from research/RELATED_WORK.md as a characterization of approach families, not as a cited benchmark claim.

Checkpointing in WORLD is the same primitive at document scope: an immutable name-to-version pin, with resume() restoring the pinned document as a new version rather than rewinding history. Two properties differ from typical framework checkpoints. The pin targets a human-meaningful work state rather than a step boundary, and resuming never rewrites the log, so the fact that a rollback happened is itself part of the auditable record. WORLD checkpoints offer no storage-level snapshotting, deduplication, or cross-machine restore. They are pins in one SQLite file.

### 3.5 Provenance

WORLD's use of the word provenance is narrower than the standard vocabulary and should not be confused with it. The W3C PROV family, including PROV-DM: The PROV Data Model (W3C Recommendation, 30 April 2013), defines provenance as a record that describes the people, institutions, entities, and activities involved in producing, influencing, or delivering a piece of data or a thing, and provides a generic, domain-agnostic data model with core concepts of entity, activity, and agent for interchanging such records between systems [8]. WORLD borrows the concern, not the model. WORLD does not implement PROV, does not serialize to PROV, and does not claim PROV compliance. In WORLD, provenance is operational and local: each handoff entity can carry source and evidence links, each log row carries a self-asserted actor label, a timestamp, and a note, and the hash chain lets a checker confirm that the row being read is the row that was appended, subject to the limits in Section 8. Metric 4 in the experiments grades a still narrower notion: whether a successor correctly identifies the source of key evidence items. Readers should keep the three senses separate: the W3C standard, the WORLD record fields, and the experiment metric.

### 3.6 Agent handoff approaches

Handoffs between agents are usually implemented as message passing within one framework, as shared scratchpads or blackboard files, or as ad hoc prompt construction in which a summary or transcript is pasted into the next agent's context. Multi-agent frameworks standardize the routing, not the content: what crosses the boundary is whatever the sending side serialized, in whatever shape it chose, with no shared schema, no verification, and no record of what was dropped. WORLD's handoff position is that the boundary object should be the shared state itself. The successor loads the same named document the predecessor wrote, with its decisions, assumptions, evidence links, constraints, and verdict history intact and checkable. Whether that position produces measurably better continuations is the empirical question of Sections 6 and 7, and the current answer is narrow.

## 4 Architecture

This section describes the current primitive in src/world. The repository also contains a legacy engine in src/world_engine with its own ledger and API. Most guarantees in this paper belong to src/world. Where a capability exists only in the legacy engine, this paper says so, because conflating the two systems would overstate the primitive (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, scope note). The design rationale for the primitive is recorded in docs/adr/0008-world-primitive.md as cited in research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md, and the research state model is in docs/state-model.md.

### 4.1 One world, one file, whole-document proposals

One named world is one SQLite file. Writers, whether agents, humans, the command line interface, or the optional HTTP server, propose whole-document JSON updates. There is no partial patch language in the commit path: a proposal is a complete candidate document. Each proposal is judged by the invariants declared for the handle through which it is submitted. The verdict is COMMITTED or REJECTED, and a row is appended to the log either way, so rejected work is evidence rather than silence. The log row records a sequence number, a timestamp from the host clock, a self-asserted actor string, a free-text note, the proposed document text, the status, the hash of the previous row, and the hash of the row itself. The row hash is SHA-256 computed over the previous hash, sequence number, timestamp, actor, note, status, and document text (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A1). Storage uses SQLite write-ahead logging mode, enabled with PRAGMA journal_mode=WAL, under which changes are appended to a separate WAL file and later checkpointed back into the database file, and readers do not block the single writer [7]. No explicit PRAGMA synchronous setting appears in core.py, so durability rests on SQLite WAL defaults (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A10). The core is implemented with the Python standard library only.

Whole-document storage is a deliberate trade, and its costs are recorded rather than minimized. Storage grows as versions multiplied by document size, with no delta encoding or compaction. In return, retrieving any committed state is simple and obviously correct: state(version=N) returns the complete document stored at that committed sequence number. The audit in experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section F rejected delta or diff-based storage for exactly this reason: full snapshots are what make state retrieval, blame, and checkpoint resume simple, and the quantified cost does not yet bind at research scale.

### 4.2 The commit path: judging, optimistic concurrency, and idempotency

The commit path in World.update has two phases whose interaction is the correctness-critical part of the system, analyzed formally in Section 5 and in docs/spec/protocol-verification.md. In the first phase, the latest committed version is read outside the write transaction, a fast-path expected_version check is performed, and invariants judge the proposal against the state that was read. Judgment is intentionally lock-free. In the second phase, inside a BEGIN IMMEDIATE transaction, the expected version is re-validated against the latest committed row, idempotency deduplication is applied, and exactly one hash-chained row is appended, except on the idempotent short-circuit or a re-validation conflict, which append no row. Atomic re-validation inside the transaction is what makes exactly-one-winner semantics hold when two writers both read the same version and both pass judgment (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md). Invariants are callables that receive the old state and the new state and raise an invariant violation to reject. Shipped helpers include no_negative, monotonic, type_stable, immutable_fields, finite_numbers, require_keys, respect_locks, and all_of (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A5).

Optimistic concurrency is exposed as expected_version. A writer that read version V submits its proposal with expected_version set to V. If another writer has committed in the meantime, the re-validation fails and the second writer must retry against the new state rather than silently overwriting the first writer's work. Without expected_version, concurrent writes are last-writer-wins, and invariant judgment may have been performed against a state that is no longer current at commit time (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section B3). That caveat is part of the architecture, not a footnote to it: invariants are guarantees about judged transitions, and they become guarantees about the committed sequence only when callers use expected_version or otherwise serialize their writes.

Idempotency is exposed as idempotency_key. A repeated key for a committed update returns the original sequence number without appending a new row, and deduplication happens inside the write transaction. Every proposal is judged first, so a repeated key cannot mask a rejected row. One explicit refusal is part of the design: the encrypted update path refuses an idempotency key outright, because a replay would orphan fresh field keys (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A4). The storage limit on idempotency, that the idempotency table is not covered by the hash chain, is treated in Section 8.

### 4.3 Time travel, history, blame, and checkpoints

Time travel is committed-only. state(version=N) returns the document at a committed sequence number and raises an error for a rejected sequence number, directing the caller to proposal(seq), which returns the full row for committed and rejected proposals alike so that rejected attempts remain auditable. history() returns per-row metadata, sequence number, timestamp, actor, note, and status, oldest first, including rejected rows, but returns no document text. blame(path) answers, for one dot-separated path into the document, which committed version last changed that value, with actor, note, timestamp, and the old and new values at that path. The command line interface provides a leaf-level diff between two snapshots. Together these operations support manual reconstruction of how a document evolved. What they do not provide, in the core, is entity-level revision history as a queryable structure. That distinction is central to the experiment record and is developed in Sections 6 and 7 and in the Prompt 3 changes below.

Checkpoints pin immutable names to versions. checkpoint(name) records the current committed sequence number under a name, and duplicate names raise an error. resume(name) appends the checkpointed document as a new version, judged by the current invariants. Resume never rewrites history, so a rollback is itself a recorded event. Prompt 3 change F3a strengthened checkpoints by recording the pinned row's hash at creation and verifying it before serving a checkpointed state or resuming from it. Section 8 states the residual limit: the pin lives in the same database, so an attacker who rewrites both the sequence number and the pinned hash defeats the pin check, and verify() still does not cover the checkpoints table (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section H).

### 4.4 Optional integrity and confidentiality layers

Signed tips provide the out-of-band anchor that the unkeyed hash chain cannot provide for itself. sign_tip produces an HMAC-SHA256 signature over the world name, sequence number, and tip hash, with the key held out of band and never written to the database. sign_tip_ed25519 and verify_tip_ed25519 provide the publicly verifiable form, using Ed25519 as specified in RFC 8032 [4]. Verification returns false when the chain no longer contains the signed sequence number and hash pair, which is what makes truncation and full rewrite detectable for a tip holder (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A7). Ed25519 key rotation is supported through a certificate signed by the old key that binds the new public key to the current tip, with verification checking the old key's signature, key consistency, and that the certified tip remains in the chain's history. Tips carry a key identifier, by default a fingerprint prefix of the key, so verifiers can route among keys. When a tip omits the identifier, verification is permissive: the identifier is a routing hint, not a security boundary, and the code and documentation say so (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section D10). The Ed25519 implementation is vendored pure Python, validated against the RFC 8032 Section 7.1 test vectors in the test suite, and is explicitly not constant-time (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section E; docs/SECURITY.md).

Per-field encryption protects selected fields rather than whole documents. The construction in src/world/fieldcrypto.py uses an HMAC-based counter-mode keystream with encrypt-then-MAC, domain-separated subkeys, and an envelope bound to associated data consisting of the world name, the field path, and the key identifier (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A11). Redaction works by cryptographic shredding: deleting the wrapped data key for a field from the field keys table leaves the ciphertext in the chain, makes the plaintext unrecoverable without the key, and keeps verify() passing. Redaction requires unwrapping with the master key first, so a wrong key shreds nothing. The construction is custom, and this paper reports it as implemented and tested (VERIFIED as implemented, in the classification of experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A11), not as independently reviewed cryptography.

Merkle inclusion proofs operate over row hashes. merkle_root computes a root over the chain, inclusion_proof(seq) produces a proof for one row, and verify_inclusion(proof) checks a proof standalone, without database access, using domain-separated internal nodes (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A12). The root is only as trustworthy as its distribution channel: an inclusion proof convinces a verifier that a row belongs to a chain with a given root, but the root itself is unauthenticated unless it was distributed or signed out of band. An independent C# verifier with no shared code reimplements chain verification by reading the database in read-only mode, so a bug in the Python verifier cannot by itself mask a broken chain (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A13).

### 4.5 The typed handoff layer and the research state model

Core storage is one opaque JSON document per version. Domain meaning lives in conventions inside that document and in the typed handoff layer in src/world/handoff.py. The handoff layer stores an AgentState under a reserved document key. In its current form, after Prompt 3 change F1, AgentState carries an objective, decisions, assumptions, evidence, open questions, and next actions. A Decision carries text and rationale plus an identifier, a status drawn from proposed, accepted, superseded, and reversed, a supersedes link to a prior decision identifier, and a list of evidence identifiers. An Assumption carries text plus an identifier, a confidence drawn from low, medium, and high, a status drawn from active, validated, invalidated, and stale, and an optional validated_by evidence link. An Evidence item carries a claim and a source plus an identifier and a kind. Open questions and next actions remain plain strings. save_state validates referential integrity within the submitted state before proposing the update: supersedes targets must exist, evidence identifiers must resolve, and a superseded decision must have exactly one successor. Violations raise before any proposal is appended, so a malformed revision graph never becomes a committed snapshot (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section C). revision_chain is a computed, read-only view that walks committed snapshots from oldest to newest, extracts one decision by identifier from each snapshot, and emits an entry for each snapshot in which the decision's text, status, or rationale changed. project_state, added as Prompt 3 change F6, is a pure function that renders a reading view in which live items appear in full and superseded or otherwise dead items appear in compact form. Its docstring states that it is a reading view, not a state to save back (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section D).

The research state model in docs/state-model.md is broader than the shipped handoff layer. It specifies Project, Task, Decision, Assumption, Evidence, Artifact, Action, StateChange, and Question entities, each with a cross-cutting provenance block. After F1, the shipped schema implements the model's Decision fields, the Assumption entity, and Evidence identifiers and kinds. The remaining entities are still draft only, core storage remains one opaque document per version, and nothing in core.py validates the full model (docs/state-model.md, implementation status note, 2026-11-04). The history of this gap matters. Before F1, the shipped AgentState had no assumptions section at all and no confidence, status, supersession, or evidence linkage on decisions. The hand-authored bundles that scored well on assumption retention in Experiment #003 used the research model's fields as Markdown prose. No code in the repository at that time validated, stored as structure, or reconstructed those fields (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, sections C and D1). F1 closed that expressiveness gap. Whether closing it improves handoff outcomes beyond the three revalidation tasks is a separate, still open question.

### 4.6 Prompt 3 changes as architecture history

Because the architecture changed after Experiment #003 in response to an audit of that experiment, the changes are recorded here as part of the system description, with full detail in experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md. F1, the handoff schema upgrade described above, with referential-integrity validation and the revision_chain view. F2, HTTP writes fail closed: with no API key configured, write routes return 401 unless unauthenticated writes were explicitly allowed in code or on the command line. F3a, checkpoint pinned_hash tamper evidence, with the residual limit stated above. F4, an optional server-side actor override: a server configured with a fixed actor replaces body-supplied actor labels on writes, narrowing but not eliminating the self-asserted actor problem. F5, streaming verify(): verify() and blame() iterate rows with a cursor instead of materializing the whole chain, with measured effects in Section 9. F6, the project_state projection view described above. F3b, binding idempotency keys into the row hash, was not done. It would change the hash format and break verification of every existing database. It remains undecided and untouched (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, sections H and J). The audit also explicitly rejected, as unjustified by evidence, a core revision-history table, replacing the vendored Ed25519 implementation, delta storage, a general role-based access control system, zero-knowledge proofs, a storage-layer fix for unverifiable additions, and executing the TLA+ specification (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section F). None of those were built.


## 5 Formal Properties

### 5.1 What was checked, and how

The commit protocol in World.update is the trust root of WORLD: every state change, every invariant judgment, and every optimistic concurrency decision flows through it. The subtle part is the read-to-lock race described in Section 4.2. The latest committed version is read outside the write transaction, so two writers can both observe version V, both pass judgment, and then serialize at BEGIN IMMEDIATE. Correctness rests entirely on re-validation inside the atomic section. The verification in docs/spec/protocol-verification.md and spec/model_check.py addresses exactly that protocol, given SQLite's BEGIN IMMEDIATE serialization guarantee.

The model represents each update as two atomic transitions. READ_JUDGE reads the latest committed sequence number and snapshot, performs the fast-path expected_version check, which ends the operation with a conflict error before judgment and appends nothing if the version is already stale, branches adversarially on the invariant verdict, and records the operation as pending. READ and JUDGE are fused because neither touches shared state, and every interleaving that a three-phase model would distinguish is preserved, since a commit can still land between another writer's READ_JUDGE and its commit step. ATOMIC_COMMIT is one indivisible step: it re-validates expected_version against the latest committed row, applies idempotency deduplication, and appends exactly one hash-chained row, or no row on the idempotent short-circuit or a re-validation conflict. resume() delegates to update() and is therefore covered. Checkpoints are out of scope for the checker because they only pin sequence numbers and never mutate the log (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md).

The checker is an explicit-state model checker in Python. It exhaustively explores the reachable states of the abstract transition system over three bounded configurations and checks six properties on every reachable state. The configurations and counts are: 165,579 states for 2 writers with 2 operations each over full domains, 935,111 states for 3 writers with 2 operations each in a race-focused configuration, and 119,565 states for 2 writers with 3 operations each in a sequential configuration, for a total of 1,220,255 explored states (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md, results table; VERIFIED in research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md, with a re-run on 2026-11-02 reproducing the state counts and property results, and a further re-run on 2026-11-04 after the Prompt 3 changes producing the identical total, as recorded in experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section E). TLA+ provides the specification language and methodology background for this style of work (Lamport, 2002) [9], and WORLD includes a TLA+ transcription in spec/world.tla written action by action against the Python mechanization. That transcription has NEVER been run through TLC. The Python checker is the executed and authoritative artifact, and no claim in this paper treats the TLA+ file as checked (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md, limits; research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md, where the fidelity of the transcription is classified as NEEDS VALIDATION).

### 5.2 The six properties

The property wording below follows docs/spec/protocol-verification.md.

P1, seq integrity: row sequence numbers are dense 1 to N, unique, and append-only. P2, chain integrity: every row's prev_hash equals the previous row's hash, over all rows by sequence number, with REJECTED rows included. P3, committed-only version: version() equals the maximum COMMITTED sequence number, or 0 if there is none, and REJECTED rows never contribute. P4, exactly-one-winner: for each expected version V, at most one operation commits with expected_version equal to V. Each committed append strictly increases the committed version, so a second contender necessarily fails the atomic re-validation. P4 is the property the whole exercise exists to establish. P5, idempotency: the idempotency table always maps each key to the sequence number of its latest COMMITTED use, and a short-circuit returns exactly that sequence number and appends no row. P6, judgment recorded: every operation that reaches the JUDGE step appends exactly one row, except the idempotent short-circuit, which appends none. A conflict error raised before JUDGE appends nothing, because the attempt was never judged and there is nothing to record.

All six properties held on every reachable state in the explored configurations (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md). The abstractions under which they held are part of the result and are stated in the verification writeup. Documents are abstracted as opaque tokens, justified because verdicts are modeled as adversarially nondeterministic, so no document content could change control flow; an invariant whose verdict depends on which writer proposed, rather than on the old and new states alone, is not modeled. Hashes are abstracted as symbolic tuples, because only chain linkage is a protocol safety property, while preimage resistance is a cryptographic assumption, not a protocol property. Timestamps, actors, notes, and canonical JSON are abstracted away because they do not influence control flow. BEGIN IMMEDIATE is modeled as one atomic step, which is SQLite's documented serialization guarantee. Workloads are bounded to 2 to 3 writers with 2 to 3 operations, on the stated justification that races, lost updates, and double winners manifest with 2 writers, with 3 writers confirming that no pairwise assumption is hidden (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md, abstractions table).

### 5.3 The checker can fail

A checker that cannot fail establishes nothing. With atomic re-validation removed through an inject_bug flag in spec/model_check.py, the checker finds a P4 violation and prints a counterexample trace in which a second writer wins for the same expected version (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md, mutation testing; VERIFIED in experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A9, pinned by a test in tests/test_protocol_model.py). The mutation resurrects the lost-update race that the re-validation exists to prevent. This paper treats the mutation result as part of the verification evidence, not as an aside: the positive result and the demonstrated ability to fail are reported together wherever the state count is quoted.

### 5.4 Model checking is not testing

The distinction matters for how much weight each kind of evidence can carry, and this paper states it precisely. Model checking, as used here, exhaustively explores the state space of an abstract model of the protocol over bounded configurations. Its strength is completeness within the model and the bounds: if a violating interleaving exists in the abstract system within those bounds, exhaustive exploration will find it, and the checker demonstrated that ability on the mutated protocol. Its limits are the model and the bounds. It says nothing directly about code paths the abstraction removed, nothing about configurations requiring 4 or more distinct writers, nothing about crashes, disk behavior, or Byzantine writers, and nothing about whether the Python implementation faithfully implements the abstract transitions outside the properties the model expresses. Testing, by contrast, executes the actual implementation on sampled inputs and sampled schedules. Its strength is fidelity to the real code, including serialization, storage, and error handling. Its limit is coverage: tests sample a vanishing fraction of possible executions, so passing tests cannot establish absence of a race in an unsampled interleaving. WORLD uses both, for different claims. The protocol claim rests primarily on the model checker, supported by threaded concurrency tests, a deterministic simulation, and a linearizability check over recorded concurrent histories (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A3). Implementation behavior under faults rests on testing and fault injection, reported in Section 7.5. Neither kind of evidence is a proof for all N. The verification writeup states the bounded result as exhaustive model checking of small configurations, justified by the small scope hypothesis that protocol bugs of this class manifest with 2 to 3 participants, and this paper repeats that qualification rather than upgrading the result to a general proof (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md, limits).

The test suite provides the testing half of the record at suite scale. The suite stood at 377 passing tests at the pre-Prompt 3 baseline and at 409 passing tests after Prompt 3 added 32 tests, run on the finished tree in 65.53 seconds in the Prompt 3 record (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section E; VERIFIED). Continuous integration runs the test jobs on Python 3.11, 3.12, and 3.13 (research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). Those counts demonstrate software behavior under tested conditions. They do not, by themselves, establish anything about agent reliability, and Section 12 returns to that separation.

## 6 Experimental Methodology

This section describes the designs of Experiment #002, Experiment #003, and the Prompt 3 revalidation study. Experiment #001 is summarized in the results, because its design was a simpler manual predecessor whose full record is in docs/experiment-001-results.md. Common features of the handoff line should be noted before the individual designs. Every handoff experiment compares three conditions derived from the same predecessor work: a structured state bundle, an independently written summary, and a raw journal or transcript. Every experiment grades continuations against a key or ground truth prepared before grading. No experiment in the line used a human grader. Every experiment used a single model family. Those shared limits are treated in Section 10.

### 6.1 Experiment #002 design

Experiment #002 ran twice: a main run on Intel (INTC) investment research and a predeclared replication on Disney (DIS), with the replication company chosen in the protocol before any results existed to prevent cherry-picking (docs/experiment-002/results.md, sections 1 and 2). In each run, a predecessor agent performed investment research under coverage requirements that guaranteed decision and revision density. The Intel predecessor produced 14 decisions, 13 assumptions each with explicit confidence, 3 genuine revisions, 4 guardrails, 6 open questions, 2 failed approaches, and 32 evidence entries (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 3). The Disney predecessor produced 11 decisions, 10 assumptions with confidence, 3 revisions, 3 guardrails, 5 open questions, 2 failed approaches, and 19 evidence entries (same source). Three handoffs were built from the same predecessor output: the WORLD state authored by the predecessor itself following docs/state-model.md, a summary written by a separate independent context that received only the journal and report and was instructed to maximize useful information for a successor without any structured schema, and the unedited journal. Independence of the summary was verified in both runs: the summary writer never received the WORLD state file, and echo scans found no schema identifiers or state model references in the summary (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 4).

Three isolated continuations, one per handoff, received the same brief, the same required output format, and no browsing, so that the measurement stayed on handoff retention rather than on new research. Outputs were blinded with shuffled labels, identifying headers were stripped, and an independent evaluator graded all continuations on 14 individual metrics against an answer key built before grading began. Metrics 1 to 10 and metric 14 used anchored 0 to 5 scales. Metrics 11 and 12 were counts of unsupported assumptions and contradictions, where lower is better. Metric 13 was an itemized loss list. Metric 14 graded output efficiency. Means of metrics 1 to 10 plus 14 were reported as reference values only, because the protocol forbade collapsing the metrics into a single score prematurely (docs/experiment-002/results.md, sections 6 and 7). Scoring limitations are part of the design record: the evaluator was an agent context, not a human; blinding in the main run had a documented residual imperfection because the evaluator inherited a parent transcript that contained the blind mapping, mitigated by relabeling through a fresh shuffle, stripping headers, and an explicit off-limits instruction with grader compliance confirmed, and improved in the replication by keeping the mapping in a local-only private file until grading completed (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 10). Sample size was 1 continuation set per condition per run, one model family throughout, and no genuine context isolation between predecessor and successor beyond procedural blinding (same source). Experiment #002 is therefore classified as DOCUMENTED and exploratory, and its means are reference values, not effect sizes.

### 6.2 Experiment #003 design

Experiment #003 was preregistered on 2026-11-03T03:33Z, before any production, summary, continuation, or grading for the experiment (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md). Amendments after that timestamp required a dated addendum, and the run log records two ground truth errata and a parity gate normalization change, all recorded before any continuation ran (same source, run log).

Tasks. Eight tasks were fixed in advance, two per category: F1 and F2 in financial research, S1 and S2 in software engineering, R1 and R2 in revision history, C1 as a constraint-heavy planning task, and C2 as a low-governance routine status control on which WORLD was not expected to win (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, tasks table). Each task had a brief, scenario materials, a continuation assignment, and a ground truth authored from a single source before production runs.

Conditions. Condition A was a WORLD state bundle following docs/state-model.md, including project context and constraints, decisions with rationale and status including superseded decisions, assumptions with confidence and status, evidence with provenance, open questions, a StateChange log, and a handoff section. The successor received only the bundle. Condition B was a natural language summary written by a separate worker that saw only the predecessor's journal and report, never the state bundle, under the instruction to preserve as much as possible with no schema. The successor received only the summary. Condition C was the predecessor's raw journal, unedited. The successor received only the journal (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, conditions).

Information parity gate. Every phrase in a task's must_mention list was required to appear in all three artifacts before that task's continuations ran, checked by an automated parity gate. A parity failure was to be fixed on the production side, never by editing ground truth, and every failure and fix was recorded. The gate passed for all 8 tasks before continuations ran, after 7 initial failures were dispositioned as hyphen normalization false positives, ground truth wording corrections recorded as errata, and one genuine summary omission fixed by revising the summary (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, run log; experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md, design as executed). The parity gate is both a control and a limitation. It guarantees that no condition wins merely because its artifact omitted a required fact. It also compresses differences between conditions, because required content, including revision elements, is forced into every artifact. Section 7 returns to that dual role when interpreting the revision history result.

Trials and independence. Three trials per task per condition gave 8 tasks by 3 conditions by 3 trials, for 72 continuations, plus 8 predecessor productions and 8 summaries. Each continuation ran in a fresh worker session, in randomized order generated with a recorded seed before runs began. Trial independence is session independence only: same platform, same underlying model family, no seed or temperature control, and exact model version not reported by the platform (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md, reproducibility). Artifacts were fixed per task and condition, so trials varied in the successor session, not in the artifact.

Scoring. Blind graders, fresh agent sessions that had produced none of the continuations they graded, scored each continuation against ground truth with per-item verdicts, per-metric scores, and a short rationale. One grader session graded each task. Condition labels were replaced by random per-task labels, with the mapping kept in a private file graders never saw. Graders also recorded a failure-cause code for each missed revision chain, chosen from data model, writing, retrieval, and interpretation causes, after scoring, by comparing the continuation to its artifact. In the event, no revision chain failures occurred and no failure-cause codes arose (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md). Blinding is imperfect by declaration: artifact format can reveal condition, so blinding hides labels and trial identity, not format (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, blinding and integrity).

Metrics. Ten metrics were fixed in the protocol (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md; operational definitions in the experiment rubric). Metric 1, decision retention: fraction of ground truth decisions recovered with correct status, with rationale required for full credit per item. Metric 2, constraint retention: fraction of constraints correctly stated and respected in proposed next actions, with any violated constraint also flagging a hard failure. Metric 3, assumption retention: fraction of assumptions recovered with fact-versus-assumption status and confidence intact. Metric 4, provenance: fraction of key evidence items whose source is correctly identified. Metric 5, revision history reconstruction: per revision chain, a 6-item checklist covering the original, the revised position, what changed, why, the authoritative version, and provenance, reported per chain and aggregated. Metric 6, factual consistency: count of contradictions, where lower is better. Metric 7, unsupported inference: count of invented items, split into confirmed fabrications, meaning items contradicted by or numerically specific beyond the materials, and unverifiable additions, meaning items absent from the key and not contradicted, where lower is better. Metric 8, task continuation quality: an anchored 0 to 3 judged rubric per task. Metric 9, information loss: the itemized complement of misses across metrics 1 to 5, with a loss count. Metric 10, context efficiency: handoff artifact words and characters per condition, and preservation, defined as the mean of metrics 1 to 5, per 1,000 words of artifact. No composite score was computed or reported.

Analysis plan. The preregistered analysis reported, per task and per condition, the mean and standard deviation of each metric across the 3 trials, with n reported everywhere, plus paired comparisons within task and trial slot, reported as mean paired differences of A minus B and A minus C with the count of pairs. The revision history subset, tasks R1, R2, and the chains in F1 and S1, was to be reported separately. No significance tests were to be performed and no significance language used, because at 3 trials per cell the design cannot support them. Effect sizes were to be reported as observed mean differences labeled PRELIMINARY. Failed, refused, or malformed trials were to be recorded and counted, never silently dropped. In execution, 72 of 72 continuations completed and were blind-graded, with zero failed, refused, or malformed trials and zero retries (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md). Sample sizes for the reported means are 24 continuations per condition for most metrics, 21 for metric 3, and 18 for metric 5 (same source).

Critical scope fact. Condition A artifacts in Experiment #003 were hand-authored Markdown bundles written by agents following docs/state-model.md (experiments/experiment-003/artifacts/*/state.md, as recorded in experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, scope note). Experiment #003 therefore tested a document schema as prose. It did not exercise the World class, the persistence layer, src/world/handoff.py, or any code path in core.py. Every experimental claim from Experiment #003 inherits that limitation, and this paper repeats it at each point where Experiment #003 results are used.

Declared limits of the design. The protocol declared in advance what the experiment could not establish: cross-model transfer, human auditability, token-level efficiency, statistical generalization, and production reliability. A positive result could support at most a claim scoped to this protocol, these tasks, and this model family, per metric (experiments/experiment-003/protocol.md, final preregistration section).

### 6.3 Prompt 3 revalidation as a follow-on study

After Prompt 3 changed the shipped code, a revalidation study asked a narrower question than Experiment #003: does state carried through the actual code path, using the new F1 schema with save_state and load_state, preserve handoff content at a level comparable to the hand-authored condition A bundles, and does the F6 projection degrade revision history reconstruction. The study is reported in experiments/prompt3/revalidation/RESULTS.md and is classified there and here as PRELIMINARY, single model family, with 3 continuations per cell and blind agent grading, and no significance claims supported at that sample size.

Design. For tasks R1, R2, and C1 from Experiment #003, the preserved predecessor production state document was mapped by the implementer into the new AgentState schema, with mapping decisions and frictions recorded in the study's mapping document, then saved through the real save_state code path and rendered two ways with one renderer: a full load_state bundle, called A-prime full, and a project_state bundle, called A-prime projected. Three fresh continuations per task per bundle type, 18 in total, ran with the Experiment #003 continuation prompts and were graded blind by fresh grader agents against the Experiment #003 rubric on metrics 1, 3, 5, 7, and 8, with metric 10 as bundle word count. The comparison figures for Experiment #003 condition A were taken from the preserved Experiment #003 analysis and score files for the same tasks (experiments/prompt3/revalidation/RESULTS.md).

Two integrity notes belong to the methodology, not to a footnote. First, the mapping from prose productions into the schema was performed by the implementer, and mapping choices shape what the bundles can carry. The mapping record documents, among other frictions, that multi-successor partial supersession is not representable under the approved validation rule of exactly one successor per superseded decision, so on task R1 a second supersession link survives only in rationale text, and that prose sections of the original productions, including provenance paragraphs, StateChange logs, and artifact lists, have no AgentState fields and were omitted from A-prime bundles (experiments/prompt3/revalidation/RESULTS.md, reading and limits; experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section I). For that reason, A-prime word counts are not like-for-like against Experiment #003 condition A word counts. The like-for-like efficiency comparison is full against projected, which share a renderer and a state. Second, continuation outputs were re-filed once after a delivery inconsistency was caught against the on-disk prompts and ground truths, and grading was restarted on a fresh blind mapping. The incident and correction are documented in the study's provenance note, and all revalidation numbers derive from the corrected set (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section I). This study is the first experiment line in the program that exercised the shipped code path.


## 7 Results

Results are reported by evidence line, strongest systems evidence and weakest generalizations kept separate. Negative findings are stated in the same subsections and at the same prominence as positive findings, following the practice of the underlying reports. No significance tests were performed in the handoff experiments, per preregistration, and no significance language is used for them in this section.

### 7.1 Experiment #001: an early clean result that did not repeat at full strength

Experiment #001, a manual run on a Block research task dated 2026-09-17, compared the three handoff formats at n equal to 1 per condition. The structured state continuation preserved 5 of 5 decisions with rationale and 7 of 7 assumptions with confidence, with zero errors (docs/experiment-001-results.md; DOCUMENTED in research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). Facts survived every format. Governance content, meaning decisions as explicit decisions and assumptions with their taxonomy intact, survived only where it was stated explicitly. The summary continuation also scored 5 of 5 overall but lost one decision as an explicit decision. The journal continuation scored 4 of 5 overall and preserved only 1 of 5 decisions with rationale under the recorded itemization (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 16, reproducing the Experiment #001 record). The direction of this result replicated in Experiment #002. Its clean sweep did not. The harder Experiment #002 design, with blind labels, a predeclared answer key, an independently written summary, and 14 metrics instead of 10 protocol measures, produced a narrower verdict in every respect (research/LIMITATIONS.md). Experiment #001 is therefore reported as DOCUMENTED background, n equal to 1, and no claim in this paper rests on it alone.

### 7.2 Experiment #002: WORLD best on governance in both runs, revision history failed in both runs

Reference means, defined in Section 6.1 as means of metrics 1 to 10 plus 14 and reported as reference values only, were as follows (docs/experiment-002/results.md, sections 7 and 11). Intel run: WORLD 3.92, journal 3.58, summary 3.33. Disney replication: WORLD 4.45, summary 4.09, journal 2.82. The WORLD-best ordering replicated across the two runs. The ordering of the two conventional alternatives did not: the journal beat the summary on Intel and lost to it by a wide margin on Disney. With n equal to 1 per run, the ordering of the conventional alternatives is instance-sensitive, and the only stable ordering claim is that the WORLD continuation preserved decision governance best in both runs (same source, section 11). All Experiment #002 results are DOCUMENTED and preliminary in character, from an exploratory design with one model family.

Where WORLD helped, stated specifically. On Intel, assumption preservation and confidence preservation each scored 5 of 5 for WORLD against 3 of 5 for both baselines. The WORLD continuation was the only one to revisit all 13 assumptions with explicit confirmed or revised verdicts and to carry every inherited confidence level, including downgrades (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 12). On Disney, provenance scored 5 for WORLD against 4 for the summary and 2 for the journal, and superseded decisions scored 3 for WORLD against 0 for both baselines; the WORLD continuation was the only one to carry evidence identifiers throughout and to name the superseded decision (same source). The strongest mechanism-level finding came from the Disney journal continuation, which renumbered every decision, assumption, and question under its own labels. That relabeling is where its governance items were lost, including an entire research thread, one decision, one question, all inherited confidence levels, and formal provenance labels. The structured labels survived into the WORLD continuation and kept the corresponding thread alive there. The grader's observation, preserved in the results record, is that relabeling is not neutral reformatting; it is where items get lost (same source, section 12). Constraint preservation was perfect or near-perfect in every condition in both runs, so structure imposed no fidelity penalty on the dimension both formats already handle well.

Where WORLD hurt, stated at equal prominence. Revision history scored 2 of 5 for the WORLD continuation in both runs. On Intel, revision history was weak in all conditions, with WORLD at 2, the journal at 3, and the summary at 2. On Disney, the journal continuation scored 4 while WORLD and the summary each scored 2. The structured state did not carry old-to-new-to-trigger revision chains into the continuation in either run. The results record calls this a replicated finding, not a gap in testing (docs/experiment-002/results.md, sections 7 and 13; research/LIMITATIONS.md). Near summary parity is the second negative result: on Disney, an independent, schema-free summary came within 0.36 of WORLD on the reference mean, 4.09 against 4.45, which keeps falsifiability condition N1 open. If a well-written summary can come that close, the case for infrastructure over convention is narrow (same sources). Third, no handoff was lossless. The WORLD continuation dropped revision chains in both runs, superseded-decision history and evidence identifiers in the Intel run, and part of one research thread in the Disney run (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 14). Fourth, on unsupported material, the Intel run recorded 0 unsupported assumptions and 0 contradictions in all three continuations, while the Disney grading recorded counts of 6 for WORLD, 7 for the summary, and 6 for the journal on the metric labeled unsupported assumptions. Those Disney counts are explicitly recorded as unverifiable against the key rather than confirmed fabrications, because a key-only grader cannot distinguish inherited detail that the key omits from invention (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 7). Contradictions were 0 in all conditions in both runs (same source).

Hypothesis reading for Experiment #002, in the terms of Section 2. Retention was supported narrowly and specifically: stable labeled governance records, meaning assumptions with confidence, provenance identifiers, and superseded decisions, survived handoffs better in these two runs, with label stability as the observed mechanism. Revision history preservation, one of the four advantages the original hypothesis claimed, was not supported; it failed twice. Summary parity remained open. Cross-model consistency and human auditability were untested by this experiment (docs/experiment-002/results.md, section 17).

### 7.3 Experiment #003: one consistent advantage, on assumption metadata

### 7.6 Systems evidence: concurrency, integrity, and fault handling

The systems evidence answers RQ4 and is independent of the handoff results. Each item below names its artifact and its classification from research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md.

Lost updates. In a preregistered experiment with scripted agents racing read-modify-write cycles, 33 of 50 trials lost updates with no concurrency control, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.52 to 0.78 for the loss rate, and 0 of 50 trials lost updates with expected_version plus retry (experiments/lost-update/RESULTS.md; preregistration in experiments/lost-update/PREREGISTRATION.md; DOCUMENTED). The agents were scripted, not language model driven, and an LLM-agent replication was planned but not run because no model API credential was available when the experiment was executed (research/LIMITATIONS.md). The claim supported is therefore about the mechanism under scripted racing, consistent with property P4, not about LLM writer behavior.

Tamper detection by fuzzing. verify() detected 500 of 500 seeded random in-place mutations, plus 450 more under alternate seeds (tests/test_verify_fuzz.py and its 2026-10-15 commit record; DOCUMENTED, with counts not re-measured in the 2026-11-02 audit, per research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). Adversarial tests pin the positive and negative boundary case by case: surgical edits to state, status, note, or actor, timestamp backdating, middle-row deletion, and row reordering are detected, while tail truncation, full consistent rewrite, idempotency-table deletion, and independently valid forks are not detected by verify() alone, each limit pinned by a test asserting that the attack succeeds (docs/SECURITY.md; experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A8). Checkpoint repointing belonged to the undetected list before Prompt 3 and is now detected at serve time under F3a, subject to the residual limit in Section 8.

Crash consistency. Fault injection recovered cleanly in 15 of 15 recorded mid-transaction SIGKILL rounds, where clean recovery means the world opens after the kill, verify() returns true, all states parse, and version() equals the highest COMMITTED sequence number (experiments/crash-consistency/RESULTS.md; DOCUMENTED as recorded). A re-exercise on 2026-11-02 ran 39 additional rounds with independent checks, including verify(), a SQLite integrity check, JSON parsing of every row, and version consistency, and found zero durability violations (experiments/crash-consistency/FINDINGS-2026-11-02.md; the underlying property is classified as VERIFIED by re-exercise in research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). The one observed failure mode in that investigation was a test setup race in which the worker had committed 0 rows before the kill on a slow disk, not a durability violation (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section A10). Crash testing covers process death only. Host-level failures, including disk loss and power loss mid-sync, and crashes during contention are out of scope (research/LIMITATIONS.md).

Stateful differential testing. Hypothesis stateful testing compared the World implementation against a reference model over 1,526 generated operations across 30 examples and found zero divergences (docs/spec/stateful-testing.md, recorded 2026-10-15; DOCUMENTED, with exact operation counts from the recorded run and not re-measured, per research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). The stateful test itself runs inside the test suite.

Independent verification. The C# verifier with no shared code accepted valid chains and rejected tampered ones in 4 tests covering a valid mixed-verdict chain, a tampered row, a broken link, and a wrong world name (tests/test_csharp_verifier.py; VERIFIED in research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md).

Soak. A soak run accumulated 1.06 million rows. Bulk writes ran at 69,714 operations per second and single writes at 2,473 operations per second in that record, latency at the median stayed flat from 0.36 to 0.39 milliseconds, and verify() processed 75,921 rows per second while using memory linear in chain length, about 659 megabytes of resident set size at 1.06 million rows under the pre-F5 implementation (experiments/soak/RESULTS.md and experiments/soak/results.json, 2026-10-15; DOCUMENTED, not re-run in the audit, per research/RESEARCH_OVERVIEW.md). ## 8 Security and Threat Model

This section follows docs/SECURITY.md, Part A, which covers the current primitive in src/world. Every guarantee and limit in the source document is pinned by an adversarial test in tests/test_redteam.py: tests whose names end in a detection marker prove the guarantee, and tests whose names record that they document a limit prove the limit by asserting that the attack succeeds. If a future change closes a limit, the corresponding limit test fails and forces the threat model document to be updated (docs/SECURITY.md).

### 8.1 Attacker model

Four attacker classes are considered. A1 has read access to the database file and sees everything that is not field-encrypted: documents, notes, actors, and rejected attempts are cleartext. A2 has write access to the database file and can run arbitrary SQL, bypassing the API entirely. A2 is the primary adversary for the chain analysis. A3 is an API-constrained writer who can only call the public surface, including update, resume, the command line interface, and HTTP, and is constrained by invariants and by the commit protocol verified in Section 5. A4 is host compromise or clock control, which is out of scope for the chain to defend, because a compromised host owns the process, the file, and the verifier. Timestamps come from the host clock and are not trusted time, although they are covered by the row hash, so backdating after the fact is detected (docs/SECURITY.md, attacker model).

Trust boundaries follow from the implementation. Between a caller and a World handle there is no authentication; invariants are the only gate, and only the invariants that handle was constructed with. Between an HTTP client and the server there is, after Prompt 3 change F2, a shared API key gate on writes when a key is configured, and an explicit opt-in requirement when none is. Between a database file holder and the chain there is only the unkeyed hash chain, which provides tamper evidence and nothing more. Between a tip holder and the chain there is keyed or public-key authenticity for the tip only, when a tip was signed and stored out of band. Between a master key holder and encrypted fields there is authenticated encryption; everyone else sees ciphertext envelopes inside otherwise cleartext documents (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section E).

### 8.2 What verify() detects

verify() recomputes every hash link over all rows in sequence order. It returns false when an attacker edits any field of any row in place, including state, status, note, actor, or timestamp, because every one of those fields is covered by the row hash. It returns false when a middle row is deleted, because the next row's previous hash no longer matches. It returns false when rows are reordered, because the links no longer line up (docs/SECURITY.md, what verify() detects). An API-constrained attacker additionally cannot commit an invariant-violating document: the attempt is appended as REJECTED evidence and the committed version does not move. Resuming an old checkpoint appends a new version and never rewrites the past (same source).

The correct one-sentence characterization is that the hash chain is tamper-evident, not immutable. It detects partial edits and accidental corruption. It does not, by itself, stop or detect a file holder who rewrites history completely and consistently.

### 8.3 What verify() does not detect

Tail truncation, or rollback, is not detected. Deleting the last N rows leaves a shorter but internally consistent chain, verify() returns true, and the version silently moves backward. Detection requires pinning the tip hash out of band (docs/SECURITY.md, limit 1). Full chain rewrite is not detected. The row hash is unkeyed, so an attacker who recomputes every link over forged history produces a chain that verifies cleanly. No better hash function fixes this limit; only a key, through signed tips, or an external anchor does (same source, limit 2). Checkpoint repointing is not detected by verify() itself, because the checkpoints table is not covered by the hash chain. Since Prompt 3 change F3a, repointing is nevertheless detected at serve time: a checkpoint records the pinned row's hash at creation, and state by checkpoint and resume() verify the pin before serving, raising an invariant violation on a repoint. The residual limit is stated plainly in the source: the pin lives in the same database, so an attacker who also rewrites the pinned hash to match the new target defeats the check, signed tips remain the out-of-band defense, and checkpoints created before F3a carry no pin and are served unverified (same source, limit 3, as amended 2026-11-04). Idempotency-table deletion is not detected. Exactly-once behavior per key depends on the idempotency table, which is not tamper-evident, so deleting a key's row lets a retry commit a second time while the chain still verifies (same source, limit 4). Forks are not detected. Copying the file and extending each copy differently yields two chains that both verify; only tip hash comparison reveals the divergence (same source, limit 5).

### 8.4 Mitigations built, mitigations open, and authorization limits

Signed tips are built. HMAC-SHA256 tips and Ed25519 tips over the same name, sequence number, and hash message close the truncation and rewrite limits for a holder who stored the tip out of band: truncation and full rewrites are detected, while honest appends after signing keep old tips valid. verify() itself is unchanged, which is why the limit tests described above remain green as documented behavior (docs/SECURITY.md, mitigation 1). Ed25519 key rotation is built, through an old-key-signed certificate binding a new public key to the current tip. Rotation bounds future forgeries. It cannot un-compromise signatures made before an old key leaked, and a rotation certified before a rollback does not validate the rewritten chain. HMAC keys rotate by a dual-signing protocol during a transition window, with no dedicated API (same source). Measured signing performance for the vendored pure Python Ed25519 implementation, about 147 signs per second and about 109 verifies per second, is recorded in Section 9 with its date and source. The implementation is not constant-time, and that caveat travels with every performance and security statement about it.

Checkpoint table inclusion in the hashed record, in the full form that would close the repointing limit at the verify() level, remains open; the serve-time half is built under F3a. Write-once or append-only storage for ledger segments, which would close the truncation, rewrite, and idempotency limits at the storage layer, remains open. Per-actor identity remains open (docs/SECURITY.md, mitigation ranking).

Authorization requires separate, blunt treatment. The actor field is self-asserted everywhere. Nothing authenticates that the named actor made the write. The row hash covers the actor string, so it cannot be edited after the fact without detection, but that is integrity of the label, not authenticity of the attribution. Any audit conclusion of the form that a named agent decided a named item rests on self-assertion (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, sections B1 and D7). There is no per-actor identity and no role-based access control. With a single shared API key, any key holder can write as any actor. Two partial changes from Prompt 3 narrow the surface without closing it. Under F2, HTTP writes fail closed: with no API key configured, the write routes for update, checkpoint, and resume return 401 unless unauthenticated writes were explicitly allowed, intended for local development. The prior behavior, a startup warning followed by open writes, is gone. Read routes stay open in every mode, by design, which is a data exposure decision that belongs in any deployment checklist, because rejected proposals, including their full documents and notes, are readable by anyone who can reach the port (docs/SECURITY.md, HTTP write authentication; experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section E). Under F4, a server configured with a fixed actor overrides any body-supplied actor on writes, so a dedicated single-purpose server's log cannot be made to name someone else. It is one shared label, not per-user identity (docs/SECURITY.md, mitigation 4 note).

Two further limits complete the model. Invariants live on the handle, not in the database. Two handles on the same world with different invariant lists judge differently, and a handle with no invariants judges nothing. The database therefore cannot name the rule set that judged a historical version. The HTTP server is the uniform enforcement point for remote writers, and that statement is true only if every writer actually goes through that server (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, section B2; research/LIMITATIONS.md). Confidentiality is not attempted by the chain and is attempted only field by field through the encryption layer of Section 4.4, whose construction is custom HMAC-based counter mode with encrypt-then-MAC in src/world/fieldcrypto.py, reported as implemented and tested, not as independently reviewed (experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, sections A11 and E).

## 10 Limitations

This section consolidates the limitations recorded in research/LIMITATIONS.md, experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md, experiments/prompt3/AUDIT.md, and experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md. Nothing in this section is softened relative to those sources.

Experimental limitations. The handoff experiments are small. Experiments #001 and #002 used 1 continuation set per condition per run. Experiment #003 used 3 trials per cell over 8 tasks, for 24 continuations per condition on most metrics. No significance tests are supportable at those sizes, and none were performed in Experiment #003 by preregistered decision. All handoff results use a single model family. Cross-model transfer is untested in every experiment in the line. Graders in Experiments #002 and #003 and in the revalidation study were agent sessions, not humans. Experiment #003 used one grader per task, reported no inter-rater reliability, and preserved raw outputs specifically so that a human can grade a subset later. No human auditability exercise has been run: no human has traced a decision from WORLD state to its evidence as an audit task, so falsifiability condition 4, verification irrelevance, and success claims about human auditing remain untested. Blinding in every experiment is imperfect because artifact format can reveal condition. In Experiment #003, the information parity gate likely compressed differences between conditions relative to unfiltered real-world handoffs, by forcing required content, including revision elements, into every artifact. Ceiling effects on metrics 2, 5, and 6 in Experiment #003 leave no room to distinguish conditions on those metrics. Predecessor productions and summaries were single instances per task, so artifact quality variance is confounded with condition. Efficiency was measured in words and characters only, never in tokens, because no tokenizer existed in the experiment environment. Most importantly for scope, Experiment #003 never exercised the shipped code: its condition A was hand-authored Markdown. The revalidation study exercised the code path on only 3 tasks, with implementer-performed mapping from prose into schema, at 3 continuations per cell.

Negative results that limit the thesis. Revision history preservation failed in both Experiment #002 runs for the WORLD condition, at 2 of 5, and then failed to discriminate at all in Experiment #003, at ceiling in all conditions. The correct status of the revision claim is unresolved and protocol-sensitive, classified as DOCUMENTED, not as supported in either direction. Summary parity remains open and largely standing: a strong summary came within 0.36 of WORLD in the Experiment #002 replication, and the summary condition matched WORLD on 7 of 9 reported metrics in Experiment #003 while beating it decisively on efficiency. Decision retention slightly favored both baselines in Experiment #003. Unverifiable additions appeared only under the WORLD condition in Experiment #003, at 12 against 0 and 0, against the registered prediction. Structured state cost 2.7 to 3.3 times the words of the baselines in Experiment #003 for preservation that was equal on most metrics. No handoff in Experiment #002 was lossless, including the WORLD handoffs.

System limitations. The hash chain is tamper-evident, not immutable, with the five undetected attack classes of Section 8.3, of which checkpoint repointing is now partially mitigated at serve time and the rest remain open. Invariants are code, not data: the database cannot name the rule set that judged a historical version, and enforcement is uniform only for writers who pass through a correctly configured server. Actor attribution is self-asserted, with no per-actor identity and no role-based access control, narrowed only by the server-side fixed actor option. Read endpoints are unauthenticated by design. Field encryption is a custom construction that has not had independent cryptographic review, and the vendored Ed25519 implementation is not constant-time. Appends serialize on a single SQLite write lock, with the measured contention plateau and latency degradation of Section 9. The design is local-first: one SQLite file per world, with no replication and no synchronization protocol, and multi-machine sharing out of scope for the primitive. Storage grows linearly with versions multiplied by document size, with no delta encoding or compaction. verify() time is linear in chain length. Whole-document proposals mean that unrelated edits share one version, one actor label, and one note, which is why entity-level revision history had to be built as a schema convention plus a computed view rather than read off the log. Multi-successor partial supersession is not representable under the current save_state validation rule of exactly one successor per superseded decision, a schema finding from real revalidation data, recorded rather than worked around (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section I). Assumption supersession and invalidation provenance likewise have no fields in the current schema (same source). Decision and assumption identifiers are not auto-assigned on save; callers supply them (same source).

Verification limitations. Model checking is bounded to configurations of 2 to 3 writers with 2 to 3 operations. It is exhaustive for those configurations and is not a proof for all numbers of writers. The TLA+ transcription has never been run through TLC, so its fidelity is NEEDS VALIDATION and the Python checker is the only executed verification artifact for the protocol. Crash testing covers process death by SIGKILL only, not host failure, disk loss, power loss, or crashes during contention. Durability rests on SQLite WAL defaults, with no explicit synchronous setting in the code. Hypothesis stateful testing, tamper fuzzing counts, and soak figures are DOCUMENTED records from dated runs that were not all re-measured for this draft, as Section 7.6 states per item.

Validation and deployment limitations. WORLD has no external users, no deployments, and no customers. Design decisions rest on the maintainer's experiments, not on observed demand. There are zero external-user conversations behind the current design (research/HYPOTHESIS.md). The project is a working research prototype at version 0.1.0, not deployed, with no hosted service, no uptime or support commitment, and no migration tooling beyond export and import of ledger rows (research/LIMITATIONS.md). The ontology has been exercised mainly on investment research tasks in Experiments #001 and #002 and on the mixed but still limited genre set of Experiment #003. Whether the work-state schema transfers to coding, operations, or scientific workflows at production depth is untested. The claim that WORLD improves agent reliability in general is NEEDS VALIDATION, and nothing in the current record changes that classification.

## 11 Future Work

Future work is ordered by what the current record identifies as decisive, not by what would be easiest to build. Each item names the gap it addresses.

A discriminating revision experiment comes first, because revision history is the point on which the two main experiments disagree and on which the original hypothesis made its strongest prediction. The design requirements are recorded in experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md, recommended next steps, and in the Prompt 3 audit: harder revision tasks with more chains per task, chains whose elements are split across sources so that no single passage states the whole chain, and no parity gate on revision elements, so that metric 5 can discriminate instead of sitting at ceiling. The experiment should also code failure causes, distinguishing a data model failure, a writing failure in which the predecessor never recorded the chain, a retrieval failure in which the successor did not use recorded structure, and an interpretation failure. Without that coding, a future metric 5 result will be as ambiguous as the current disagreement. Whether the F1 schema, with explicit status, supersession, and evidence linkage, plus the computed revision_chain view, changes revision outcomes under those harder conditions is the single most informative next measurement in the program. It should be run through the shipped code path, not through hand-authored Markdown, so that the scope defect of Experiment #003 is not repeated.

Human grading of the preserved subset comes second. Experiment #003 preserved raw outputs so that a human can grade a 25 percent subset, 18 continuations, after the fact (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md, limitations and recommended next steps). Human grading would check the agent graders, especially at the metric 5 ceiling, where leniency is one of the recorded candidate explanations for the null result. It would also provide the first evidence of any kind on human auditability if paired with a trace task in which a human follows a decision from state to evidence and the points where the trail breaks are recorded. Until some version of that exercise runs, human auditability remains NEEDS VALIDATION and should be described that way.

Cross-model runs come third. Every handoff result in this paper is single model family. The standing research plan records the next evidence step, when a model API key exists, as cross-model revalidation together with a human-graded subset (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, section J). A cross-model design should run the predecessor in one model family and the successor in another, in both directions, on the Experiment #003 tasks or their harder successors, because the central hypothesis is about work moving between different participants, and same-family results cannot establish that the benefit, where it exists, is a property of the representation rather than of shared habits within one family.

The F3b decision comes fourth, because it is the one remaining engineering item that would break existing databases. Binding idempotency keys into the row hash would make the committed row itself prove its key binding and would close the idempotency-table deletion limit at the chain level, but it changes the hash format, so every existing database would fail verify() under new code. Alternatives that avoid the break, including a parallel chained table for key bindings and periodic signed tips covering the table, are weaker or operational rather than cryptographic. The Prompt 3 record leaves F3b undecided and untouched until it receives its own review (experiments/prompt3/REPORT.md, sections H and J). Future work on this item is a decision procedure, with migration cost stated in advance, not an implementation to be folded into unrelated changes.

An external validation protocol is pending and is fifth precisely because it depends on the items above. External validation here means a preregistered protocol under which participants outside the project, using their own tasks and their own graders, test whether structured work-state preserves decision-relevant information better than their current handoff practice, with the falsifiability conditions of Section 2 stated in advance, including summary parity and tool sufficiency, meaning Git plus documents plus a tool protocol as a serious baseline rather than a straw baseline. No such protocol has been run, no external user has audited a real chain, and recruitment, task selection, and grading independence for such a study remain to be designed. This paper records the protocol as pending rather than describing it as planned in detail it does not yet have.

Lower priority items follow from the limitation record. At-scale re-running of the soak after F5 would replace the 2026-10-15 memory figure with a current one. A persisted invariant registry would let the database name the rule set that judged a historical version. Per-actor identity, if a multi-actor deployment requirement ever becomes concrete, would address the self-asserted actor limit at its root; the Prompt 3 audit rejected a general access control system as premature in the absence of such a requirement, and this paper retains that judgment. Schema extensions for multi-successor partial supersession and for assumption invalidation provenance would close gaps found on real revalidation data. None of these items should be presented as substitutes for the first three evidence steps.

## 12 Conclusion

What the evidence establishes can be stated precisely, in four claims at four different strengths.

First, as a systems claim, VERIFIED: the WORLD commit protocol, as abstracted in spec/model_check.py, satisfies properties P1 to P6 over 1,220,255 explored states in three bounded configurations, and the checker demonstrably detects the removal of its central safeguard by producing the P4 counterexample (docs/spec/protocol-verification.md). The implementation that realizes that protocol passes 409 tests, detects the tamper classes listed in Section 8.2 under adversarial tests and seeded fuzzing as DOCUMENTED, recovers cleanly from process-kill fault injection as DOCUMENTED and re-exercised, and eliminates the measured lost updates for scripted racing writers as DOCUMENTED. The same record establishes the boundary of the systems claim with equal precision: verify() alone does not detect tail truncation, full consistent rewrite, idempotency-table deletion, or forks; actor attribution is self-asserted; invariants are per-handle code; and no result here is a proof for unbounded configurations.

Second, as a handoff claim, PRELIMINARY and EXPERIMENTALLY OBSERVED in one design: under the preregistered Experiment #003 protocol, with an information parity gate, 8 tasks, and a single model family, a structured state bundle preserved assumption confidence and fact-versus-assumption status better than a summary or a journal, by paired differences of positive 0.221 and positive 0.233 on metric 3, through the visible mechanism that explicit fields survive while prose mentions degrade to partial credit (experiments/experiment-003/REPORT.md). The Prompt 3 revalidation adds, also PRELIMINARY, that state carried through the shipped F1 code path preserved the graded content on tasks R1, R2, and C1 at a level comparable to the hand-authored bundles, with no revision metric regression from projection (experiments/prompt3/revalidation/RESULTS.md). That is the full extent of the positive handoff claim this record supports.

Third, as a set of negative and null claims, at the same prominence: structured state showed no measured advantage in Experiment #003 on decisions, constraints, provenance, revision history, contradictions, or judged quality; it was the least efficient condition on all 8 tasks by a factor of about 2.7 to 3.3 in words; it was the only condition under which unverifiable additions appeared; revision history preservation failed for WORLD in both Experiment #002 runs and then failed to discriminate in Experiment #003, leaving the revision claim unresolved and DOCUMENTED as protocol-sensitive; and summary parity largely stands, in both the near-parity replication result of Experiment #002 and the 7 of 9 metric match in Experiment #003. Any summary of WORLD that omits this paragraph misrepresents the record.

Fourth, what the evidence does not establish, stated as plainly as the positive claims: it does not establish that WORLD improves agent reliability in general, that structured state helps across model families, that humans can audit WORLD records more effectively than transcripts in practice, that the ontology transfers beyond the tested task genres, that any real multi-agent system or real user benefits from WORLD in production, or that the revision representation now shipped performs well under revision tasks hard enough to discriminate. Each of those claims is NEEDS VALIDATION. The contribution of the current record is narrower and, on the evidence presented, real: a judged, verifiable work-state primitive whose commit protocol is model-checked within stated bounds, whose security limits are pinned by tests that assert them, and whose one measured handoff benefit, preservation of assumption metadata, is specific enough to be checked, replicated, or refuted by the future work in Section 11.
